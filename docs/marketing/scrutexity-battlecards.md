## AuditGPT Battlecard — Objections That Actually Exist

Scope note: these are the four objections we can currently defend with evidence. This is not a competitor map — it's a live sales tool for a $99-$799 product with zero closed customers. Update this chapter the first time a real prospect raises an objection not listed here; don't add hypothetical ones.

### 1. "How is this different from Truli?"

What Truli actually is: an AI compliance platform that scans label copy, marketing content, and social media against FDA/FTC substantiation standards, matched to a brand's specific product formulation — built for food, beverage, and supplement brands.

The honest answer: Truli is the closest thing to a real competitor we've found, and we should say so if asked directly rather than dodge it. The differences:

- Vertical: Truli is built around a product's formulation data (ingredients, nutrients) — it's CPG-shaped. AuditGPT is built around a service business's website and AI-agent claims — no formulation to check against, different evidence sources (page text, not lab data).
- Scope: Truli doesn't appear to audit AI customer-service agent transcripts. That's the Agent Guardrail Audit half of AuditGPT, and it's not something a claims-scanning tool for supplement labels is built to do.
- Depth of proof, not breadth of claim: we're not trying to out-scan Truli across more surfaces (label + social + ads). We're going deeper on evidence traceability per claim — grounding verifier rejects anything that can't be matched to source text, sealed audit trail with hashing/timestamping. If a prospect is a supplement brand, Truli may genuinely be the better fit — say that.

Do not say: "we're a broader/better version of Truli." We haven't earned that claim yet with zero customers. Say what's actually true: different vertical, different claim surface, and a transcript-audit capability they don't appear to have.

### 2. "Why wouldn't I just have my lawyer review this?"

What's actually true: A lawyer (or a firm like KR Law, which does exactly this — places AI marketing claims into risk categories and builds substantiation cases) gives you authoritative legal judgment. That's real, and AuditGPT is not a substitute for it — we say this explicitly in our positioning ("not legal advice, not compliance certification").

The honest answer:

- Cost and cadence: legal review is billable-hour, point-in-time, and expensive enough that most SMBs only do it reactively (after a complaint or before a big launch), not continuously. AuditGPT is cheap enough ($99 first review) to run before you're worried, and cheap enough to re-run when your site changes.
- Division of labor, not replacement: AuditGPT's job is to find the claims and show the gap — what's claimed, what evidence exists, what's missing. That's the triage a lawyer would otherwise spend billable time doing manually. We hand them a shorter, more specific list to review, not a legal opinion.
- What we don't do: we don't tell you whether a claim is legally defensible, we don't certify compliance, and we don't give clinical or legal conclusions. If a prospect's real need is a legal opinion, the honest move is to say a lawyer is the right next step after our review, not instead of it.

Do not say: anything implying AuditGPT reduces legal risk or liability. That's a claim we can't substantiate about our own product, which would be a bad look for a claim-support company.

### 3. "Why wouldn't I just ask ChatGPT to check my website?"

This is probably the single most common objection for a $99 product, and it deserves a real answer, not a dismissive one.

The honest answer:

- A general-purpose chat model will happily tell you a claim "looks fine" or "seems overstated" based on its training data and general judgment. It has no mechanism forcing it to check the claim against your actual page text or to refuse an answer it can't ground in evidence — it can be confidently wrong, and there's no audit trail showing its work.
- AuditGPT's Grounding Verifier is specifically built to reject any extracted claim that can't be matched to source page text — the point isn't "AI reviewed my site," it's "every finding is traceable to a specific line on a specific page, with a sealed, timestamped record of what was checked." If a prospect (or their lawyer, or an investor, or a regulator) asks "how do you know that's a problem," we have an answer that a raw ChatGPT session doesn't produce.
- Deterministic fallback and a Safety Scanner that blocks banned phrases, guarantees, and unsafe framing before output ships also aren't things a person gets from typing a prompt into a general chat interface — those are pipeline guarantees, not one-off judgment calls.

Do not say: "ChatGPT can't do this." It can approximate it. The honest differentiator is evidence traceability and a repeatable, auditable process — not a capability gap in the underlying model.

### 4. "This isn't urgent enough to pay for right now."

What's actually true: we have zero paying customers and no closed-won evidence that urgency converts to payment yet. This objection is the one we're least equipped to argue against with proof today — so the honest answer leans on regulatory context, not manufactured urgency.

The honest answer:

- Real, current enforcement context we can point to without overstating it: the FTC's "Operation AI Comply" initiative has brought more than a dozen actions against unsubstantiated AI-capability claims, and enforcement has continued into 2026; health and GLP-1/weight-loss claims are a named current priority; New York's AI content-disclosure law took effect in June 2026. This is real and dated, not fear-based framing — we state it plainly, we don't threaten.
- We are explicitly not allowed to use fear-based or FTC-threat framing per our own positioning rules, so the honest pitch is "here's what's actually happening in enforcement right now, here's a cheap way to see where you stand" — not "you will get sued."
- If a prospect says "not urgent," the correct move is not to manufacture urgency — it's to note the $0 Free Claim Snapshot exists specifically for exactly this hesitation, and let them self-select into the $99 review once they see what the snapshot finds.

Do not say: anything implying a specific business is at legal risk, or naming their claims as likely violations. We flag support gaps and safer framing, not verdicts.
