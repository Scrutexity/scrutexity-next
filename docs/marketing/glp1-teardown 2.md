## Why We Reviewed These Pages

The GLP-1 market is moving fast. Every week, another med-spa, wellness clinic, or telehealth provider launches a weight-loss landing page. And every week, those pages are written under the same pressure: get the offer out before the clinic down the street does.

The problem is that GLP-1 advertising sits at the intersection of three active enforcement zones:

- **FTC weight-loss advertising scrutiny** — The FTC has made numeric outcome claims, before/after testimonials, and weight-loss guarantees a priority enforcement category. The 2023 *Weight Loss Advertising Task Force* continues active review.
- **FDA medication advertising standards** — "FDA approved" has a specific meaning for specific medications and specific indications. Compound semaglutide and tirzepatide do not carry FDA approval, yet the line between brand-name and compound language is blurred on many pages.
- **State AG consumer protection actions** — Attorneys general in New York, California, Texas, and Florida have opened or escalated inquiries into weight-loss clinic advertising, particularly around compounded GLP-1 claims and undisclosed side effects.

We ran our claim audit tool on 10 GLP-1 clinic websites to see what's actually being said — not in the fine print, but in the hero copy, the testimonial sections, and the price-comparison tables where patients actually look.

**Five claim patterns appeared consistently across the sample.** Here they are, what they look like in practice, and what the support picture actually shows.

## Pattern 1: Guaranteed Results — Numeric Outcome Promises Without Cohort Data

**The pattern:** A specific weight-loss number or percentage presented as what patients will achieve, without disclosing what the supporting cohort looked like.

**What we saw:**

- "Lose 15–20 pounds in your first month"
- "Average 12% body weight reduction"
- "80% of patients lose significant weight within 90 days"

**Why this is a problem:** The FTC's *Guides for Weight Loss Advertising* are explicit: numeric outcome claims require competent and reliable scientific evidence, typically a well-designed clinical study with a subject population relevant to the target audience. The issue isn't that the numbers are wrong — it's that the claim implies a guaranteed outcome while the data backing it is either absent or drawn from manufacturer trials for brand-name medications (not compound formulations or the specific program being advertised).

**Real example we analyzed: Ro.co/weight-loss**

Ro's weight-loss page is one of the better-disclaimed examples in the sample. They use the phrase *"Lose 20% body weight on average with Zepbound\*"* — and the asterisk correctly points to the SURMOUNT-1 manufacturer trials (tirzepatide). The disclaimer is present and references a named study.

However, the claim still sits in the hero section, above the fold. A patient who reads *"Lose 20% body weight"* and then clicks "Get Started" isn't necessarily parsing the asterisk footnote about clinical trial averages. And if Ro is prescribing Zepbound (brand-name tirzepatide), the study link holds. If the page is also referencing compounded alternatives — a growing pattern for telehealth providers — the same data doesn't transfer.

**The safer approach:** Pair numeric language with a named source and the relevant cohort. *"In the SURMOUNT-1 clinical trial (N=2,539), patients taking tirzepatide lost an average of 20.9% body weight over 72 weeks. Individual results vary."*

Our existing claim audit page flags phrases like *"Lose 20 pounds in 30 days"* as a priority issue — numeric outcomes without cohort data or qualification criteria.

## Pattern 2: FDA Approved Confusion — Conflating FDA-Approved Drugs with Compounded Formulations

**The pattern:** Using "FDA approved" descriptively on pages that offer compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide, or failing to distinguish between the FDA status of the brand-name drug versus the compounded version.

**What we saw:**

- "FDA-approved GLP-1 medications for weight loss"
- "Our providers prescribe FDA-approved semaglutide"
- Mixing photos of Ozempic/Mounjaro pens with "Now offering compounded semaglutide" in the same section

**Why this is a problem:** The FDA has been explicit: compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. The FDA's September 2024 and March 2025 enforcement updates on GLP-1 compounding make clear that stating or implying FDA approval for compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide is misleading. Even when a page correctly links to brand-name trials, presenting those trial results alongside a compounding offer creates an implied equivalence that the public record does not support.

**The regulatory risk is real.** In March 2025, the FDA sent warning letters to multiple compounding pharmacies and telehealth operations specifically for misleading GLP-1 advertising. The letters targeted pages that failed to clearly distinguish between the FDA-approved status of brand-name drugs and the unapproved status of compounded versions.

We flagged *"FDA approved for weight loss"* in our 10-phrase audit because it applies only to specific medications and specific indications — compound semaglutide does not carry the same approval.

**The safer approach:** Use precise language. *"Brand-name Wegovy (semaglutide) is FDA approved for weight management. Compounded semaglutide is not FDA approved, but is available through licensed compounding pharmacies when the brand-name drug is in shortage."* Then separate the offers visually so the patient understands what they are choosing.

## Pattern 3: No Side Effects / Safe for Everyone — Minimizing Known Side Effect Profiles

**The pattern:** Downplaying or omitting the side effect profile of GLP-1 medications, or presenting them as universally safe.

**What we saw:**

- "Safe for all patients"
- "Well-tolerated with minimal side effects" (without listing what those are)
- Zero side effect disclosure near the sign-up CTA — buried in a terms page or absent entirely
- "No downtime" used interchangeably with "no side effects"

**Why this is a problem:** GLP-1 medications have documented, non-trivial side effects. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and abdominal pain affect a significant portion of patients in clinical trials. More serious risks — pancreatitis, gallbladder events, thyroid C-cell tumors (in animal studies), and gastroparesis — are listed in prescribing information for brand-name medications.

The FDA prescribing information for semaglutide (Wegovy/Ozempic) includes a boxed warning for thyroid C-cell tumors. For tirzepatide (Zepbound/Mounjaro), gastrointestinal adverse reactions were the most common reason for discontinuation in clinical trials.

When a page says "no side effects" or "safe for everyone," the contrast with the FDA prescribing information is stark. This isn't about alarm — it's about the gap between the claim and the public record.

**Compounding adds another layer.** Compound GLP-1 medications may have additional risks related to sterility, potency variability, and dosing errors. The FDA has issued multiple alerts about adverse events from compounded semaglutide specifically.

Our audit flagged *"No side effects"* and *"Safe for everyone"* as two of the 10 most common risky phrases — both are directly contradicted by prescribing information.

## Pattern 4: Before/After Without Context — Testimonial Framing Without Typical Result Disclosure

**The pattern:** Testimonial carousels with dramatic before/after imagery or transformation stories, without disclosing whether the results shown are typical or telling the viewer what typical results actually look like.

**What we saw:**

- Five-photo before/after carousels with no accompanying text about average or expected results
- "I lost 45 pounds in 3 months!" testimonials without any indication of whether that experience is representative
- Patient stories framed as "transformations" with no mention of program duration, adherence requirements, or concomitant lifestyle changes

**Why this is a problem:** The FTC's *Endorsement Guides* and *Guides for Weight Loss Advertising* treat before/after claims in weight-loss advertising as a high-scrutiny category. If a testimonial presents a specific outcome, and that outcome is not typical, the advertiser must clearly disclose what the generally expected result is.

The FTC's 2023 update to its Endorsement Guides reinforced this: if you show an exceptional result, you cannot assume viewers will understand it's exceptional. You must state what typical results are.

**The compounding healthcare complexity:** Beyond FTC rules, presenting before/after imagery for a medical weight-loss program — where results depend on adherence, dosing, lifestyle, and individual physiology — without context is also a medical advertising concern. The combination of "transformation" language and a weight-loss medication creates a claim that requires evidentiary support from both a marketing and medical standpoint.

Our audit flag on *"Before/after transformation framing"* calls out FTC requirements for clear disclosure of typical vs. atypical results.

## Pattern 5: Clinically Proven Unsupported — Citing Clinical Proof Without Naming Studies

**The pattern:** Using phrases like "clinically proven," "doctor recommended," or "scientifically formulated" without citing a specific study, journal, trial identifier, or named recommending physician.

**What we saw:**

- "Clinically proven results" with no hyperlink, footnote, or study reference
- "Doctor recommended" generically — no attribution to any specific physician or medical body
- "Backed by science" used as a header above a pricing table
- "Thousands of patients trust us" without any source for the patient count

**Why this is a problem:** *"Clinically proven"* is a high-evidence claim. It asserts the existence of clinical data supporting the specific offering. Without naming the study, the trial, the journal, or the outcome measure, the claim is untestable by the reader. The same applies to *"doctor recommended"* — which physician, in what context, for which patient population?

In a regulatory context, "clinically proven" for a compounded medication is particularly fraught. The clinical trials for semaglutide and tirzepatide were conducted on brand-name, FDA-approved formulations with specific dosing, purity, and delivery mechanisms. Applying that evidence base to compounded versions, which have not undergone the same trials, requires a level of bridging argument that most pages do not attempt.

**Ro's approach stands out here too.** Their Zepbound claim cites SURMOUNT-1 by name and links to the data. That level of specificity — naming the trial and what it measured — is what "clinically proven" should look like. Most pages in our sample did not reach that bar.

Our audit flags *"Clinically proven results"* as a phrase that requires a named study, journal, or trial — without citation, it's unsupported marketing language.
